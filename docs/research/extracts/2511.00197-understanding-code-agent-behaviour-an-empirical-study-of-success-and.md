## Core results

**1. Failed trajectories are longer than successful ones, in every agent × benchmark cell — but by a
modest margin with almost total range overlap.** (RQ2, Table `tab:trajectory_stats`; Finding 4.)
Step counts (a *step* = one thought, action, **or** result event; §Methodology for RQ2):

| Benchmark | Agent | Succ avg | Fail avg | Succ min–max | Fail min–max | Succ sd | Fail sd |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lite | OpenHands | 61.01 | 79.90 | 15–297 | 12–296 | 46.60 | 60.63 |
| Lite | SWE-agent | 158.36 | 178.31 | 60–537 | 73–351 | 54.91 | 58.42 |
| Lite | Prometheus | 87.16 | 136.47 | 25–192 | 33–691 | 34.62 | 100.79 |
| Verified | OpenHands | 54.05 | 98.62 | 18–295 | 24–297 | 40.42 | 80.81 |
| Verified | SWE-agent | 151.95 | 180.10 | 58–447 | 66–377 | 56.14 | 59.00 |
| Verified | Prometheus | 146.59 | 220.92 | 28–546 | 11–715 | 73.93 | 133.70 |

Relative excess length of failures (paper's own normalisation, §Agent profiles): SWE-agent +12.6 %
(Lite) / +18.5 % (Verified); OpenHands +31.0 % / +82.5 %; Prometheus +56.6 % / +50.7 %.
**No threshold, no significance test, and no effect size are reported**, and success/failure ranges
overlap nearly completely (OpenHands Lite: successes run to 297 steps, failures to 296).

**2. Variance separates outcomes more sharply than the mean.** Failure standard deviation is ~1.3×
success for SWE-agent, 2× for OpenHands on Verified, and ~2.9× for Prometheus on Lite (100.79 vs
34.62). The authors read this as two failure populations: a fast-failing cluster near the median and
a long, unpredictable tail (§Agent profiles; violin figures 2–3).

**3. More steps per success correlates with *less* divergent failure — the opposite of "shorter is
better".** SWE-agent, whose successes run 2–3× longer than OpenHands', has the smallest failure
inflation and sd gap. The authors conjecture (not measure) that "incremental reasoning styles (many
small steps) may reduce catastrophic failures compared to short, aggressive approaches" (Finding 5).

**4. Fault localisation is a funnel, and file-level success is nearly universal — including in
failures.** (RQ3, Table `tab:faultloc`.) Correct-file rate, successes: 93.5 / 95.9 / 97.3 % (Lite,
OpenHands / SWE-agent / Prometheus), 93.6 / 92.2 / 95.8 % (Verified). Failures: 79.8 / 81.4 / 72.5 %
(Lite), 62.7 / 59.3 / 67.1 % (Verified). Exact-function match collapses to ~21–34 % even in
successes; exact-hunk to 2.7–8.4 %, with a further 9.3–12.6 % within 5 lines. Findings 6–7: correct
file is necessary but not sufficient; failure is a fine-grained-reasoning problem, not a repository-
navigation problem.

**5. Wrong-file trajectories do not fail faster.** On Verified, 35–40 % of failures never touch a
gold file, yet failing-trajectory lengths are comparable across Lite and Verified — "agents spend an
equivalent amount of time exploring wrong and correct file paths" (Finding 8). The paper's proposed
remedy is *earlier detection of an incorrect file*, not a length cap (§Discussion).

**6. Qualitative failure patterns (case studies, n = 9).** Unproductive reasoning loops that the
agent cannot exit: Prometheus hits recursion limits repeatedly and "was not able to re-evaluate its
hypothesis even though it entered multiple loops" (sympy-12236); OpenHands patches escalating,
increasingly unrelated errors rather than revisiting the root cause. Finding 2: "Limiting recursion
is not enough… Agents need mechanisms to abandon unproductive reasoning loops." Finding 1:
repository-aware context gathering (Prometheus' knowledge graph) yields architectural-pattern
awareness and the right abstraction level — but the same mechanism is what strands it in search.
Finding 3: defensive programming can substitute for domain knowledge.

**Not measured, despite being adjacent:** no breakdown of steps into navigation/read vs edit vs test
categories; no repeated-read or duplicate-file-access metric; no token counts, dollar costs, latency,
or context-window figures anywhere in the paper; no give-up/abandon classifier; no per-phase timing
of the five-phase framework. Efficiency appears only as prose ("SWE-Agent fails faster, which is
desirable for performance, resource efficiency").

## Method and setting

Observational trajectory study, no intervention and no training. Three open-source agents on
SWE-Bench Lite (300 issues) and Verified (500): **OpenHands** (CodeAct 2.1, Claude Sonnet, Oct 2024),
**SWE-agent** (Claude 4 Sonnet, May 2025), **Prometheus** (GPT-4o on Lite; Devstral Medium 2507 on
Verified) — Table 1. OpenHands/SWE-agent traces come from the public SWE-Bench experiments bucket;
Prometheus traces from the authors' own runs. Per-agent parsers normalise the logs into a
thought/action/result step array (Table `tab:parser_summary_auto`). RQ1 is a manual read of 9
trajectories (3 uniformly sampled per agent's uniquely-solved set), segmented into Diagnosis →
Reproduction → Hypothesis/Plan → Implementation → Verification, treated as a loop on re-attempt.
RQ3 diffs each candidate patch against the gold patch at file / function / hunk level (hunk gets a
0.5 "within 5 lines" partial credit). Trajectory counts implied by the RQ3 table: Lite 124/173,
170/129, 75/200 success/failure; Verified 265/228, 333/167, 191/155. Single run per issue — no
seeds, no repeats, so run-to-run variance is unestimated. Threats section concedes the step
extraction "may be subjective" and that comparisons are made "mainly in relative terms".

Scale calibration for us (derived, not in the paper): 3 steps ≈ 1 tool-loop turn, so their whole
SWE-bench trajectories are ≈ 20–60 turns for OpenHands and ≈ 50–60 turns for SWE-agent. Our
code-writer's *orientation phase alone* (14–65 turns) is the size of an entire trajectory in this
dataset; our 20–200-turn sessions sit at or above SWE-agent's upper range.

## Relevance to P1–P4

**P1 (context is the cost).** No bearing. The paper reports no tokens, no cost, no cache behaviour.
Its "wasted computation" framing is qualitative.

**P2 (sessions grow; the longest dominate).** The one directly relevant result, and it is weaker
than the headline reads. Length *is* associated with failure in all six cells, but at a derived
Cohen's d of ≈ 0.35–0.70 (my computation from the table's means and sds: OpenHands Lite 0.35,
Verified 0.70; SWE-agent 0.35 / 0.49; Prometheus 0.65 / 0.69). Under a normal approximation — which
the right-skewed violins violate — that is a single-feature AUC of roughly 0.60–0.69: better than
chance, far short of a kill switch. The result that *does* transfer cleanly is #2: the failure
distribution has a heavy tail, so the cost blow-ups and the failures are the same sessions. And
Finding 5 is an explicit warning against reading "long session" as "bad session" — the agent with
the longest successes had the best-behaved failures.

**P3 (every session rebuilds the same picture).** Only indirect. Finding 1 says repository-aware
context gathering (a pre-built codebase knowledge graph) is what let Prometheus pick the right
abstraction level on issues neither competitor solved — support for durable, curated repo knowledge
over per-session rediscovery. But the same agent shows the worst failure-length variance, and its
signature failure is *getting stuck inside context gathering*. The paper never measures re-reading,
so it cannot price our per-dispatch re-read of plan.md and friends.

**P4 (what smaller reads cost in grounding).** Offers one instrument, not an answer. File-level
localisation is ~93–97 % in successes and still 59–81 % in failures, so "did the agent look in the
right file" has almost no discriminating power once the file is easy to find (Lite); the informative
band is function/near-hunk. If we wanted an early grounding proxy, the paper's evidence says measure
*whether the first edit lands in the right region*, not *how much was read*.

## Interventions this paper supports

- **Instrument trajectory length per role and alert on the tail, don't cap it** (Findings 4–5,
  Table `tab:trajectory_stats`). Record turns and context per session in `state.json`; treat a
  session past the role's own historical p90 as a *review* signal. Expected effect: catches the
  heavy-tail sessions that dominate spend. Loses nothing (pure observation); a hard cap would lose
  the long-but-successful runs the ranges show exist.
- **Detect the wrong-region signal rather than the long-session signal** (Findings 7–8). A cheap
  check after the first edit — does the touched file set intersect the plan phase's `Target:` /
  attachments? — is the discriminator the paper actually argues for; length is not. Effect
  direction: catches the 35–40 % of Verified failures that never touch the right file while still
  burning full-length trajectories. Risk: legitimately unforeseen files get flagged, so it must
  steer (prompt back), not kill.
- **Give code-writers an explicit abandon-and-re-hypothesise instruction with a bounded retry**
  (Finding 2, sympy/pylint case studies). The observed failure is patching cascading symptoms
  without revisiting the hypothesis. Cost: a re-hypothesise round that would have converged anyway.
- **Keep durable repo-architecture notes as a dispatch artefact** (Finding 1). Cheaper than the
  agent rediscovering conventions; the paper's caution is that a rich retrieval surface invites
  unbounded search, so bound what the artefact permits, don't just enlarge it.
- **Do not chase exact-location precision in acceptance criteria** (Finding 6; §Discussion): even
  successes match the gold hunk only 2.7–8.4 % of the time. Approximate edits succeed.

## Applicability caveats

- **No Claude Opus, no maximum-effort configuration.** Two of three agents run Claude Sonnet
  (OpenHands on a 2024 CodeAct 2.1 build); the third runs GPT-4o / Devstral. Prompted only —
  nothing here is trained, so nothing needs a training pipeline to adopt.
- **Task shape differs on the axis that matters.** SWE-bench is one autonomous agent, one issue,
  one shot, with the benchmark's own tests as ground truth. Our unit is a *phase* of a planned slice
  with a deterministic gate and a reviewer downstream, and the exploration we care about happens
  before the first edit inside an otherwise-successful session. The paper's outcome variable is the
  whole attempt; it has no analogue of a within-session orientation cost.
- **"Steps" mixes thought, action and result** and is agent-parser-dependent; the authors compare
  "mainly in relative terms" and warn the extraction is subjective. Absolute step numbers should not
  be ported to turn budgets.
- **Single run per issue, no statistical testing.** The length effect is a mean difference over one
  sample with overlapping ranges. Prometheus on Verified is a different base model from Prometheus
  on Lite, so its cross-benchmark deltas confound agent and model.
- **Benchmark quirk worth noting:** failures are *worse* at file localisation on Verified (59–67 %)
  than on Lite (72–81 %), which the authors find surprising and cannot explain.

## Briefing check

S5's note asks for "the measured effect of shaping what a file read returns, and what trajectory
studies say about navigation versus editing and about length as a failure signal."

- **Shaping file reads: this paper says nothing.** There is no read-shaping, no context-pruning, no
  tool-output experiment. That half of S5 rests entirely on SWE-Pruner.
- **Navigation versus editing: the note overstates what is available.** The paper never reports
  step counts by category. The only decomposition is a manual five-phase reading of 9 trajectories,
  with no numbers attached. The nearest quantitative statement is inferential, from the RQ3 joint
  frequencies: "trajectories are generally able to find the correct file, and spent most of their
  efforts exploring within the file" — an interpretation of patch-target combinations, not a
  measurement of exploration actions.
- **Length as a failure signal: supported, with a caveat the note should carry.** "Across all
  configurations, failed trajectories are longer on average and have a wider distribution than
  successful ones" (Finding 4) holds in all six cells. But the paper offers no threshold and never
  tests significance, the ranges overlap almost entirely, and Finding 5 cuts the other way — "a
  higher absolute number of steps in successful trajectories correlates with less divergent
  failures". The paper's own prescription in §Discussion is not a length cap but "earlier detection
  of an incorrect file". Filed correctly under S5; the usable claim is narrower than "length
  predicts failure".
