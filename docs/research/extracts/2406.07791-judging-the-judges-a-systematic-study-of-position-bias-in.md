## Core results

15 LLM judges (GPT-4/4o/4-Turbo/3.5-Turbo/o1-mini; Claude-3.5-Sonnet/3-Opus/3-Sonnet/3-Haiku; Gemini-1.5-pro/1.0-pro/1.5-flash; Llama-3.3-70B/3.1-405B/3.1-8B) on MTBench (8 tasks) and DevBench (14 tasks), pairwise + list-wise, >150k instances (Table 1, Table 2). Key effect sizes: (1) capable judges have Repetition Stability >0.85, best ones >0.95 — position bias is systematic, not noise (Sec. 4, "not mere random"). (2) Position Consistency (PC) and preference direction (primacy/recency, metric PF) vary sharply by judge and task — e.g. GPT-4/GPT-3.5-Turbo flip from recency-preferred (MTBench) to primacy-preferred (DevBench); Claude-3.5-Sonnet PF=0.01 on MTBench vs 0.22 on DevBench (Sec. 4, Table 2). (3) Strongest driver: answer-quality-gap (δq) — a parabolic relationship where near-equal-quality candidates produce the lowest PC/PF (most bias), large quality gaps produce near-perfect consistency (Sec. 4, Fig. "Judges_Fig2"). (4) Prompt/output length is only weakly (barely) significant, via stepwise regression, and only for PF (Sec. 4, Table 1). (5) Agreement analysis: >80% of 15 judges agree on >50% of instances ("easy"); <2% are high-disagreement ("hard," where bias concentrates); majority voting across capable judges is argued reliable for >95% of instances (Appendix "Agreement Analysis").

## Relevance to A/B/C

- **A (planner deep-dive sizing)**: None direct — paper doesn't study effort allocation or task-complexity routing for generation, only judgment of already-generated candidates.
- **B (comment churn / claim-weakening reviews)**: None direct — paper studies position bias in *choosing between two candidate answers*, not single-artifact critique of one document's wording. The weak-length-effect finding is tangential at best (doesn't establish verbosity drives false-positive review findings).
- **C (work amplification from individually-fair findings)**: Directly relevant. The δq finding — judges are least reliable precisely when candidates are close in quality — maps onto the "will→may" pattern: marginal findings (small stylistic quality deltas) are exactly the regime where any automated judgment, including a reviewer's, is statistically least trustworthy per this paper's data, even though each finding looks individually defensible.

## Supported interventions

1. **Swap-and-compare / order randomization** for any step that picks between candidate outputs (e.g., multiple coder attempts, multiple fix proposals) — backed by the RS/PC methodology itself: single-order judgments are shown unreliable without a swap trial (Sec. 4).
2. **Majority vote across repeated or multi-model judgment** for close calls — backed by the disagreement analysis showing >95% reliability via consensus among several capable judges (Appendix).
3. **Use estimated quality-gap/disagreement as a routing signal**, not just the finding itself — since near-tie cases are where bias concentrates, a design that treats "is this a marginal call?" as a first-class routing input (deprioritize/batch marginal findings rather than auto-fix each) is directly supported by the parabolic δq result.

## Applicability caveats

- Models are 2024-era (no Opus 5/current frontier); direction of results may not transfer to today's stronger judges.
- Task domains are single-turn Q&A (MTBench) and software *design-document* evaluation (DevBench: UML/architecture), not iterative multi-round code review or long-horizon agentic loops — no data on repeated-fix-cycle dynamics.
- Metrics (PC/PF/RS) apply to *comparative* "pick the better candidate" judgments; they don't directly model a single reviewer critiquing one artifact and raising discrete findings, which is our loop's actual reviewer pattern.
- Purely observational/post-hoc; no mitigation method is tested, so effectiveness of the interventions above (swap/majority-vote) is inferred from consistency data, not from a bias-reduction experiment in this paper.

## Briefing check

Confirmed, with a scope nuance: this paper is squarely about position bias in comparative (pairwise/list-wise) candidate selection, so "relevant to candidate-selection design" is accurate and well-supported. It is less directly about single-document reviewer critique (our current B/C pain points) — its strongest applicability is if/when the loop adds a step that judges between multiple generated candidates (plans, fixes, or outputs), where order-randomization and majority-vote guidance would transfer most cleanly.
