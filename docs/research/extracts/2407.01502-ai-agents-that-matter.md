## Core results

1. **Trivial baselines match SOTA coding agents at a fraction of the cost** (HumanEval, 164 tasks,
   mean of 5 runs; §2.3 + Appendix §8 table). Baselines with *no* agent architecture: *retry*
   (re-prompt the same model up to 5× at temp 0 when the supplied example tests fail), *warming*
   (same, temp ramped 0 → 0.3 → 0.5), *escalation* (Llama-3-8B → GPT-3.5 → Llama-3-70B → GPT-4 on
   failure). Accuracy (min–max over 5 runs) / total USD for all 164 problems:

   | agent | accuracy | cost |
   |---|---|---|
   | Warming (GPT-4) | **93.2** (92.1–93.9) | **$2.45** |
   | Retry (GPT-4) | 92.0 (91.4–92.7) | $2.51 |
   | Escalation | 85.0 (84.1–85.4) | **$0.27** |
   | LDB (GPT-4) | 93.3 (92.1–94.5) | $6.36 |
   | LDB (Reflexion, GPT-4) | 92.9 (91.5–95.1) | $7.26 |
   | LDB (GPT-3.5) | 80.2 (78.7–82.9) | $0.63 |
   | Reflexion (GPT-4) | 87.8 (86.0–90.9) | $3.90 |
   | LATS (GPT-4) | 88.0 (82.3–91.5) | $134.50 |
   | GPT-4 zero-shot | 89.6 (87.8–90.9) | $1.93 |

   Measured: "no significant accuracy difference between our warming strategy and the best-performing
   agent architecture"; Reflexion and LDB cost >50% more than warming, LATS >50×; escalation strictly
   dominates LDB (GPT-3.5) — higher accuracy at <½ the cost. At substantially similar accuracy, **cost
   differs by almost two orders of magnitude**. The June-2023-model robustness check (§8.2) reproduces
   the ordering (warming 90.6 / $3.88 vs LATS 83.5 / $360.02). **Conjectured, not measured:** System 2
   methods (planning, reflection, debugging) may still pay on harder tasks than HumanEval, e.g.
   SWE-bench; the claim is only that the question "remains open" because nobody tested these baselines.

2. **Joint accuracy–cost optimisation halves variable cost at equal accuracy** (HotPotQA; §3.2 +
   Appendix §9 table). Optimising *prompt token count* jointly with accuracy — Optuna multi-objective
   search over per-module temperature, number of few-shot examples, which examples, formatting
   instructions on/off — versus stock DSPy: GPT-3.5 **53% lower variable cost** ($0.376 → $0.174 per
   200-task eval), accuracy 0.495 → 0.509; Llama-3-70B **41% lower** ($0.643 → $0.374), accuracy
   0.617 → 0.601. Optimisation is a *fixed* cost ($2.7 / $3.8), repaid after ~1,350 tasks (1,275
   GPT-3.5, 1,332 Llama-3-70B); past that, variable cost dominates "by orders of magnitude".

3. **Retrieve-what's-needed vs read-everything** (NovelQA; §4.1 + Appendix §11 table). GPT-4 over
   whole novels (50k–1M words) vs GPT-4 + RAG (10 chunks × 1000 chars per question): accuracy
   **67.81 vs 67.89** — indistinguishable — at **$99.80 vs $52.80**. But the benchmark asks *all*
   questions about a novel in one prompt; asked one at a time, long-context costs **≈21.9× more**
   ($1.115 vs $0.051 per QA on a new novel) because the novel is re-processed per question. The
   benchmark therefore understates RAG's advantage roughly tenfold. **Run once** (cost).

4. **Reproducibility gaps swamp claimed gains** (§6 + Appendix §12). Reported → reproduced: LATS
   (GPT-4) 94.4 → 88.0; LDB (Reflexion, GPT-3.5) 95.1 → 88.9; Reflexion (GPT-4) 91.0 → 87.8. The
   **GPT-4 zero-shot baseline was reported at 75.0, reproduced at 89.6** — understated by 14.6 points,
   more than any agent's claimed gain over it. Causes: silent subsetting (LATS drops 4 problems,
   Reflexion 3, STeP 8 WebArena tasks); LATS scored against only *some* true test cases per problem,
   worth **3 points** of inflation; LATS and STeP marked failed tasks successful; WebArena's Reddit
   clone rate-limits, so task *order* changes results (2/129 tasks affected in one run, 30/129 in
   another). "Many reported accuracy scores were above the maximum of five runs we performed, and the
   reported baselines were in some cases lower than the minimum of five runs."

5. **Holdouts mostly absent or at the wrong level** (§5, Tables 1–2, Appendix §10). 17 benchmarks
   surveyed against four generality levels (distribution-specific / task-specific / domain-general /
   fully general), each demanding a different holdout (in-distribution samples / OOD samples / held-out
   **tasks** / held-out **domains**): **7/17 have no holdout and no plan for one; of the 10 with
   holdouts only 5 sit at the appropriate level.** (Table 1's numerators are lost in this copy's
   PDF→Markdown conversion; denominators are 1/6/8/2.) STeP tops WebArena at 35.8% by hardcoding
   per-task policies (e.g. append `/user/user_name` to the base URL) — accuracy that would collapse on
   held-out tasks.

6. **Human-in-the-loop is unmeasured and probably large** (§5.2, citing Shi et al. 2024, not measured
   here): simple human feedback took GPT-4 from 0% to over 86% on hard programming problems.

## Method and setting

Re-evaluation of published agents plus three new baselines and one optimiser. **No training or
fine-tuning anywhere** — prompting, sampling and orchestration only; all code released MIT.

- **HumanEval arm:** 164 single-function Python problems, LDB's modified version (adds example tests
  to the 3/164 that lack them). `gpt-3.5-turbo-0125`, `gpt-4-turbo-2024-04-09`, plus June-2023 GPTs
  and Llama-3-8B/70B. Short horizons: LDB ≤10 iterations, LATS ≤8 with expansion factor 3, Reflexion
  ≤2, baselines ≤5 attempts. **5 runs per agent**, mean accuracy and mean total cost, min/max and
  Student-t 95% CIs (n=5) in the appendix. Cost = dollar cost of API input+output tokens at dated
  per-million prices; an accuracy-vs-*time* frontier is reported too. Pareto frontier = points
  non-dominated on mean cost and mean accuracy, constrained convex (two frontier agents can be mixed
  with probability p, so the hull is achievable).
- **HotPotQA arm:** DSPy multi-hop retrieval over ColBERTv2 / 2017 Wikipedia, 100 train / 200 eval,
  fixed seed, 5 runs, scored on retrieving all ground-truth documents; GPT-3.5 and Llama-3-70B.
- **NovelQA arm:** multiple-choice subset, GPT-4, `text-embedding-3-large`. One run.
- **WebArena / survey arms:** re-running STeP; paper-and-repo survey of 17 benchmarks.

Everything is single-model API calls in short loops — no long tool loops, no prompt caching, no
multi-agent hand-offs, no repository-scale tasks.

## Relevance to P1–P4

**P1.** The paper's thesis is ours in generic form: accuracy without cost is uninterpretable, because
cost is unbounded and buyable. Cost is defined as **dollars**, and §4 insists downstream evaluations
also publish **input/output token counts** so anyone can recompute at current prices. It does not
decompose input into cache-read/cache-write — caching does not feature — so it says nothing about our
63/19/18 split. Transfer gap: our cost is dominated by *re-reading*, theirs by *re-sampling*.

**P3.** NovelQA is the closest published analogue: re-processing the corpus per query costs ~22× what
retrieving the needed slice costs, at indistinguishable accuracy (67.81 vs 67.89). But that is
multiple-choice QA over prose, one call per question, one run, GPT-4 — and crucially the *questions
were known*. It says nothing about a reader who does not yet know what to ask, which is exactly a
code-writer's orientation phase.

**P4.** This is the paper's methodological core, and its answer is procedural, not empirical: decide
by plotting both axes and asking whether the cheaper variant is *dominated*. Escalation is the
pattern in miniature — 4.6 points below GPT-4 zero-shot but 7× cheaper, and on the frontier because
nothing beats it on both axes. Its own comparisons rest on **5 runs per configuration** with min/max
and Student-t CIs, and it states that agent evaluations are usually run **once**, which is why the
field's error bars are missing and why several published numbers fell outside its 5-run range. It
offers **no power analysis and no minimum-n rule**; 5 is what they could afford, not what they derived.

**P2** — only obliquely: LATS's 50× cost blow-up comes from expansion factor × depth, one architecture
choice producing huge cost at no accuracy gain. No per-session context-growth data.

## Interventions this paper supports

- **Report every loop variant as an (accuracy, cost) pair and keep a frontier, not a leaderboard**
  (§2.3, §8.1). Extend `slice_cost.py` / close-out to emit both axes under a named variant label, so
  variants are judged by dominance rather than impression.
- **Publish token counts beside dollars** (§4: prices move, providers differ). For us the same holds
  for cache multipliers — record input / cache-read / cache-write / output per session so a pricing
  change doesn't invalidate the record.
- **Price the dumb baseline before shipping the elaborate mechanism** (result 1: retry and warming
  match SOTA and nobody had tested them). Analogue: before a curated-context pipeline, measure
  read-nothing-extra, read-only-the-diff, and plain retry of a failed phase. The paper's finding is
  that this step is routinely skipped and, when taken, often ends the argument.
- **Treat prompt size as an optimisation target under an accuracy constraint, paid for once**
  (result 2: 53% / 41% variable-cost cuts at equal accuracy by minimising prompt tokens). Our analogue
  to "few-shot examples" is the fixed prefix and the standing registers: search over what can be
  removed while a fixed acceptance signal holds. The ~1,350-use break-even framing transfers directly
  — at ≈30 sessions/slice, prefix trimming amortises almost immediately.
- **Retrieve rather than re-read where the task is comprehension** (NovelQA). Loses nothing measurable
  *on that task type* — but the comparison held the questions fixed and known.
- **Hold out slices; expect a curated-read variant to be fitted to whatever you tuned on** (§5: agent
  developers overfit "even unintentionally"). If the read list is tuned by watching 30 slices it is
  fitted to those 30. The prescribed holdout level for a domain-general claim is *tasks* — for us,
  slices from a project or subsystem never used while tuning.
- **Standardise the harness before comparing** (§6: reproduced numbers moved 3–14 points purely from
  script differences, dropped items and order-dependence). Fix the gate command, the slice set and the
  accounting script first; record which slices were excluded and why.

## Applicability caveats

- **Models:** GPT-3.5, GPT-4(-turbo), Llama-3-8B/70B. **No Claude, no Opus-class or reasoning model.**
- **Task type:** HumanEval is single-function generation *with example tests supplied* — the cheapest
  possible verification oracle, which is why retry works at all. Our slices have a gate but no
  per-attempt oracle for design quality; the authors themselves concede System 2 may pay on
  SWE-bench-class work. The other two arms are QA, not code.
- **Horizon:** 2–10 iterations. Nothing observes a 20–200-turn tool loop, context growth, compaction,
  or session hand-offs.
- **Caching absent from the cost model.** Our cheapest path (cache read at 0.1×) has no counterpart,
  so "fewer tokens ⇒ proportionally less money" is *more* true in their setting than ours.
- **Statistics:** n=5 per configuration over 164 *repeatable* items. Our slices are unique and not
  re-runnable at will, so their repeat-the-run variance control does not transfer; a paired design
  over the same slice set is the nearest substitute, and the paper offers no guidance for it.
- **No training required** — everything transfers in principle at the prompt/orchestration level.

## Briefing check

The note — "the cost/accuracy-Pareto methodology … for comparing a smaller-read variant fairly (Q6)"
— is **supported**; this is the right paper for it. Load-bearing quotes: "an agent is on the Pareto
frontier if there is no other agent that has significantly better performance on both dimensions
simultaneously" (§2.3); "For substantially similar accuracy, the cost can differ by almost two orders
of magnitude" (§2.3); "useful agent evaluations must control for cost — even if we ultimately don't
care about cost and only about identifying innovative agent designs. Accuracy alone cannot identify
progress because it can be improved by scientifically meaningless methods such as retrying" (§2.3).

Two things the note under-sells. First, the most transferable *result* for us is not the method but
NovelQA (§4.1): equal accuracy at ≈1/22 the cost once queries are asked one at a time. Second, half
the paper is about not fooling yourself — the GPT-4 baseline reported at 75.0 that reproduces at
89.6 — which is the sharper warning for an in-house A/B where we own both arms.

**One flag:** "cost-controlled" here means *reporting and comparing on both axes and demanding
dominance*, **not equalising spend between arms**. Nowhere does the paper run two agents at a matched
budget. Reading "compare at equal cost" as this paper's prescription imports something that is not
there; the prescription is that neither arm may be called better unless it wins on one axis without
losing on the other, or the loss is stated and accepted.

**The lead's transfer question, answered with what the paper does and does not license:**

- *Held fixed:* the task set (same slices, no silent exclusions — §6 and the LATS/Reflexion/STeP
  subsetting), the scoring procedure and its script, model versions and dated prices (§8.1 records
  exact model ids and per-million rates), and the harness. **Cost is not held fixed** — it is measured
  and plotted.
- *How to report:* mean accuracy and mean dollar cost per arm with min/max and CIs across runs, **plus
  raw input/output token counts** so dollars can be recomputed later (§4); add latency as a third axis
  if it matters (§8 does this for HumanEval).
- *Minimum evidence for kill/keep:* the operative bar is **dominance** — keep the smaller-read variant
  if nothing beats it on both axes; kill it if the current loop wins on accuracy at equal-or-lower
  cost. Their own empirical bar was **5 independent runs per arm** with Student-t 95% CIs, and they
  refuse to call a difference real when ranges overlap ("no significant accuracy difference between
  our warming strategy and the best-performing agent architecture"). They give **no** rule for n, and
  warn that one-run evaluations (their own NovelQA arm) cannot support any claim about variance. For
  ~30 non-repeatable slices the honest reading is: this paper licenses the *shape* of the decision
  (dominance on two axes, stated CIs, held-out slices) but not a sample-size claim — n=30 unpaired
  heterogeneous tasks is thinner evidence than their 5 runs × 164 paired items, and should be reported
  as such.
