## Core results

Controlled tag-model experiment (Sec. 3.2, LLaMA3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct fine-tuned on QwQ-32B-Preview-generated Low/Medium/High-effort CoTs, 1.3K NuminaMath problems): High effort underperforms Low on easy tasks despite far more tokens. MATH500 Level-1 breakdown (App. 13): LLaMA3.1-8B-Tag 92.09%(Low)→87.44%(Med/High); Qwen2.5-32B-Tag 97.67%(Low)→96.74%(Med/High). Low effort is consistently best on GSM8K; Medium/High better on AIME2024/harder items — the optimal effort is task-dependent (Sec. 3.2, points 2-3).

Mechanism (Sec. 3.3, 100-problem sample, GPT-4o-judged): the number and proportion of erroneous reasoning rounds rises monotonically with reasoning effort. Loss-masking erroneous steps during SFT beats training on all tokens (Fig. 1). Outright removing erroneous-step examples helps GSM8K but hurts MATH500/AIME2024 (App. 16) — reflection/correction ability is itself learned from erroneous rounds, so the fix isn't "never include errors," it's "don't over-train on them."

Self-consistency signal: the effort level with best accuracy on a benchmark also produces the fewest distinct answers across 5 samples (Table on distinct answers) — convergence tracks optimality.

TOPS method (Sec. 4-5, Qwen2.5-32B): generating at multiple efforts and self-selecting the shortest correct response for SFT beats random-selection at identical data volume: 95.82/91.48/43.33 (GSM8K/MATH500/AIME2024) vs. 95.00/90.16/39.33, using far fewer tokens on GSM8K (412 vs. 938). A prompting-only replication without any fine-tuning (App. 15, QwQ directly prompted Low/Med/High) shows the same task-dependent pattern.

## Relevance to A/B/C

**A (planner deep-dives regardless of size)** — Directly relevant. Core finding: excess effort actively hurts accuracy on easy tasks, not just wastes tokens (Sec. 3.2). Importantly, TOPS achieves difficulty-adaptivity via generate-then-select, not upfront classification — this differs mechanistically from the "grade complexity first, then route" approach the operator says already failed.

**B (comment churn)** — Weak/indirect. The erroneous-rounds mechanism concerns reasoning tokens produced during generation/training, not persisted artifacts (code comments) reviewed later. Transfer is by analogy only, not measured here.

**C (work amplification)** — None directly measured. The paper never studies multi-round review loops. The generic principle — more output volume correlates with more error content, and correcting after the fact (masking) beats blanket removal — is suggestive but not evidence about aggregate review-fix cycles.

## Supported interventions

1. Replace upfront complexity grading with outcome-based escalation: run cheap/short effort first, escalate only when the result is uncertain or fails checks — backed by TOPS's shortest-correct selection outperforming random (Sec. 5.2) and by the prompting-only replication (App. 15).
2. Use answer/output convergence as a stop signal: if independent short attempts agree, that's evidence the task didn't need deeper effort (Table on distinct answers).
3. Don't binary-suppress flagged issues; downweight/mask rather than delete — App. 16 shows removing error-containing examples entirely costs capability on hard cases versus masking just the erroneous spans.
4. Cap "polishing" of already-correct output: iterative SFT that only lengthened/refined outputs didn't reliably improve results (Sec. 5.3).

## Applicability caveats

Models are Qwen2.5-32B/7B and LLaMA3.1-8B, with QwQ-32B-Preview as teacher — not Opus-class frontier agentic models. Domain is almost entirely math (GSM8K/MATH500/AIME2024); one small, noisier non-math check (App. 17, 7B only). Most results are training-time (SFT/DPO) effects, not pure inference-time prompting — only App. 15 tests prompting alone. Scale is modest (1.3K seed, 50K generation, 100-problem error analysis, AIME2024 = 30 problems with ±3-6pt std). Published Feb 2025 (updated Oct 2025); predates whatever overthinking mitigations current frontier reasoning models may already have.

## Briefing check

Confirmed, with a mechanistic nuance. The paper does establish task/difficulty-dependence of optimal thinking length and that easy-task overthinking is disproportionate. But it's more specific than "disproportionate compute": excess CoT length actively *degrades accuracy* on easy tasks (not just wastes budget), and the causal driver is that longer CoTs accumulate proportionally more erroneous reasoning rounds that get trained on — not verbosity per se. Update the briefing note to add: harm operates via error-density growth with length, and the fix that generalizes (masking/selecting, not deleting) differs from simple length-capping.
