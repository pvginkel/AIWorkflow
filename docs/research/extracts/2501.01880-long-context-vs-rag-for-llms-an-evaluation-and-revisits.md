## Core results

1. **LC beats RAG overall by ~7 EM points, on questions that genuinely need the context.** Filtered
   full set (§5.2 counts 13,628; §3.3 says 13,651): LC **56.3 %** correct vs RAG **49.0 %**. LC
   exclusively answers **>2,000** questions RAG misses; RAG exclusively answers **~1,300** — "almost
   10 %", which the authors call "not a small ratio … retrievers cannot be simply replaced by
   long-context LLM". Loose setting (EM plus F1 tie-break): LC better on **3,433**, RAG better on
   **1,843** — a wider gap, read as LC also being strong on long open answers. *Measured.*

2. **Retrieval family decides whether retrieval is competitive: summarisation-based ≈ LC,
   chunk-based lags.** Phase 1 (sample set, 1,000+ questions) ranks BM25 / Contriever / OpenAI
   text-embedding-3-Small (chunk), Llama-Index tree-index and sentence-window (index), RAPTOR
   (summarisation). **RAPTOR best at 38.5 % correct**; index-based beats chunk-based. Within
   index-based, tree-index scores 82 "RAG Only" vs window-parsing's 91 while "RAG Better" is
   near-identical (234 vs 237) — read as the strict metric undervaluing tree-index on long answers.
   Qualitatively: RAPTOR is strongest where whole-document understanding is needed (research papers);
   chunk-based fails when facts straddle chunks; index-based is good at dialogue.

3. **Source type flips the answer. Document-like → LC; dialogue-like → RAG.** §5.3 / Fig. 6 (EM): LC
   excels on **Wikipedia and stories**; RAG is better on **dialogue-related sources** and
   **comparable on papers/reports**. Stated mechanism: dialogue, papers and reports are *naturally
   segmented* (turns, sections), so the right segment is easy to retrieve. The authors caution their
   Wikipedia contexts were lengthened by injected noise, which "partially simulates the RAG process"
   and may bias *against* RAG. **Inconsistency to note:** the appendix (loose setting, Fig. 9) puts LC
   ahead on "Wikipedia, course websites, and papers/reports" and RAG ahead on "dialogue-based **and
   story-based**" sources — the opposite placement of *stories*. Only dialogue→RAG and Wikipedia→LC
   are consistently reported; papers/reports and stories are unsettled.

4. **Question type flips it too.** Fig. 7 (EM): LC better on **fact-based "Who / Where / Which"** (the
   answer sits in a dense region); RAG **comparable on open-ended "How"/"Why"** needing synthesis; RAG
   **outperforms LC on "Other"** — mostly general yes/no questions. The proposed cause (LC treats
   wh-words as search keywords and is more familiar with their phrasing; retrievers use them poorly)
   is explicitly a **hypothesis**.

5. **Chunk count and size: few, long, in original order — survey consensus the authors adopt, not
   test.** Table tab:insights / §2.3: top-**5 to 10** chunks is the sweet spot, **>20 degrades**
   [xu2023retrieval]; too many chunks harms [OP-RAG]; longer units help [LongRAG]; passage count shows
   rise-then-drop [jin2024long]. **Original document order beats score order** [OP-RAG]; high-scoring
   chunks at front *and* back also helps [jin2024long]. No chunk sweep of their own; RAPTOR picks
   top-k nodes under a token budget by collapsed-tree traversal.

6. **The generator barely matters.** GPT-4o vs GPT-4-Turbo across BM25 / tree-index / RAPTOR: RAPTOR
   best under both, GPT-4o marginally ahead everywhere. Conclusion: "the retrieval method plays a
   larger role … than the specific generation model". Two models only; more were in progress.

7. **The failure modes are asymmetric** (§5.6 case study). RAG's most frequent error is **failing to
   retrieve, then refusing** ("The context does not provide …"); its second is **misreading partial
   context** (a birthday linked to the wrong person because the bridging sentence straddled a chunk
   boundary). "LC rarely reports a lack of context." LC's characteristic error is **question
   misinterpretation** — locating the right sentence but answering the wrong question ("how" for
   "where") — attributed to reasoning degraded by noise.

8. **The "revisits" — what the authors changed about how this is measured.** (a) **Filtering:** prior
   comparisons never removed questions answerable from parametric knowledge; GPT-4o plus strict EM
   removes them here (19,188 → 13,651 needing context). (b) **Scale:** prior studies used ~200
   questions per dataset over 6–8 datasets while LC and RAG agree on ~60 % of questions [Self-ROUTE];
   this work expands ~10× from the datasets' original sources, no LLM-generated questions. (c)
   **Retriever coverage:** of 9 surveyed studies, 5 varied the retriever, only 2 varied chunk size, and
   **none went beyond chunk-based retrieval** — RAG was systematically under-represented in every prior
   comparison. (d) **Reconciliation:** LongBench's "shorter and more chunks is better" is an artefact
   of weaker models then and of never testing units >1000 tokens — not a real contradiction. (e)
   **Attribution of disagreement:** whether retrieval helps a long-context model tracks **model size
   and open/closed** — it helps 4k but not 16k/32k 7B models [LongBench] yet helps 70B/43B at all
   lengths [xu2023retrieval]; closed models keep improving with RAG to ~100k–128k while several open
   models peak at 16k–32k then decline [leng2024, LongBench v2]. (f) **Context relevance is the
   overlooked variable:** a synthetic long context (relevant passages concatenated plus injected noise)
   is *itself already RAG output*, so feeding it whole is "long-context RAG" and re-chunking it biases
   against RAG; 6 of 9 studies use synthetic contexts.

9. **Cost: the paper reports none of its own.** The only cost claim anywhere is a *survey* row —
   Self-ROUTE: "LC consistently outperforms RAG, but RAG has lower cost". §6.3 "Computational Cost" is
   about the expense of *running experiments* across models and datasets, not inference tokens. §6.2
   names "Short RAG vs Long Single Input" as the setting where one *should* analyse cost — a proposed
   framework, no results. **No accuracy-per-token or accuracy-per-dollar curve exists in this paper.**

## Method and setting

Single-turn short-answer **QA**; no agentic loop, no tool use, no code. 12 long-context QA datasets
(NQ, 2WikiMultihopQA, HotpotQA, MuSiQue, MultiFieldQA, NarrativeQA, QASPER, QuALITY, Coursera,
TOEFL-QA, MultiDoc2Dial, NovelQA). Three phases: retriever bake-off on the 1,000+ sample set; best
retriever (RAPTOR) vs LC on the ~13.6k full set with the **same LLM generating in both arms**;
partition into LC-only / RAG-only / LC-better / RAG-better. LC = whole available context in one
prompt, **truncated from the back** past the window. Identical instruction both arms: *"From the
context: [context], answer the questions briefly with no explanation."* Metrics: strict **Exact
Match**; loose = EM plus a bag-of-unique-tokens **F1** tie-break. Generators **GPT-4o** (primary) and
GPT-4-Turbo; GPT-4o also does the filtering. **Nothing is trained or fine-tuned** — off-the-shelf
retrievers, RAPTOR's summary tree built once per document by an LLM. **No Claude model is evaluated**
(Claude appears only in the survey of long-context models). Code and data:
github.com/lixinze777/LC_VS_RAG.

## Relevance to P1–P4

**P1 (context is the cost).** No direct bearing — no token accounting, price, or latency. Its
contribution is negative but useful: the field's headline "LC beats RAG" is an **accuracy** claim, and
the one cost claim in the literature it surveys points the other way (RAG cheaper, LC more accurate).
Do not let a cost conclusion ride along when citing this for "reading beats retrieving".

**P2 (sessions grow; the longest dominate).** Weak transfer. The LC arm is one shot at a fixed context
truncated from the back — no growth dynamic, no accumulating turn history, no retained thinking. The
nearest relevant finding is that LC stayed strong on Wikipedia contexts *deliberately padded with
irrelevant passages* (§5.3). But those windows fit GPT-4o, our doc-writer reaches 438k, and the same
survey records several models degrading past 32k. Not evidence that our largest sessions are safe.

**P3 (every session rebuilds the same picture).** Strongest transfer, via result 2. **Hierarchical
summarisation-based retrieval (RAPTOR) is comparable to reading everything; chunk-based retrieval —
which is what a grep-and-read-the-hit loop is — is the family that lags.** If we replace per-session
re-reading, the substitute the paper endorses is a pre-built hierarchical **summary layer over the
artefacts**, not literal fragments. Result 6 adds that a stronger generator does not compensate for a
poor retrieval layer.

**P4 (what smaller reads cost in grounding).** This paper is the P4 experiment in QA form, and the
answer is not "retrieval is fine": **-7.3 EM points overall, and ~10 % of questions answerable only by
RAG** — the loss is real and **two-sided**, so routing by condition beats a global choice. The
transferable part is result 7's asymmetry: a context-starved agent **refuses or answers from partial
evidence** (our code-writer lacking a file; our reviewer flagging an absence that isn't one), while a
fully-fed agent **misreads the question** (our reviewer answering a nearby question instead of the
acceptance criterion). Those need different instruments — the first shows as gate-red and refuted
findings, the second as on-topic-but-wrong findings, which we do not measure.

## Interventions this paper supports

- **If we cut re-reading, cut it with summaries, not chunks.** A per-slice hierarchical digest (plan →
  phase → done-record, each level summarised) is the RAPTOR shape — best retriever, 38.5 %, comparable
  to LC — whereas grep hits are the chunk shape, the worst family. *Loses:* per result 7, whatever the
  summary elides becomes unanswerable, and the failure looks like a refusal or a confident partial
  answer, not an obvious gap.
- **Route by artefact shape, not globally.** Naturally segmented sources favour retrieval; cohesive
  sources favour reading whole (result 3). Our append-only, turn-structured artefacts (done-records,
  review files, close-out entries) are the dialogue-like class; a coherent design doc or a single
  file's implementation is the document-like class. *Loses:* the paper's own placement of
  papers/reports and stories is self-contradictory, and that ambiguous middle is where plan.md and
  diffs sit.
- **Keep any retrieved set small and in document order.** Top-5–10 units, long units, original order,
  never >20 (result 5) — applies to how an Explore sub-agent's return is shaped and to any tool-output
  wrapper. *Loses:* nothing measured here; survey consensus, not this paper's experiments.
- **Don't expect a stronger model to rescue a bad context.** Generator swap moved results marginally;
  retrieval method dominated (result 6). Supports spending on what the dispatch carries rather than on
  the model dial — consistent with the settled "no effort tiering".
- **Phrase acceptance criteria as locatable factual checks.** LC's edge concentrates on
  "Who/Where/Which" and its characteristic error is answering an adjacent question (results 4, 7);
  general yes/no framing is the class where LC was *worst* relative to RAG. *Loses:* the mechanism is
  the authors' conjecture, and the "Other" bucket is partly an EM artefact of short yes/no gold
  answers.

## Applicability caveats

- **No Claude, no code, no agents.** GPT-4o and GPT-4-Turbo only, short-answer QA scored by EM/F1 over
  token sets. Nothing about diffs, plans, multi-file edits, or tool loops.
- **Retrieval here is a fixed pipeline; ours is model-driven.** The RAG arm never lets the model choose
  what to fetch next. Our agents *are* the retriever (Read/Grep over 20–200 turns) and then read into a
  long window — a hybrid the paper never evaluates. "RAG loses 7 points" cannot be read as "our
  tool-loop reading loses 7 points".
- **Single-turn, one question, no state** — no horizon, no hand-off, no compaction question.
- **Benchmark quirks cutting both ways.** The parametric-knowledge filter makes the set deliberately
  harder than realistic use; the Wikipedia contexts are synthetic noise-padded concatenations the
  authors say bias against RAG; EM under-credits long open answers (hence the loose setting); the
  headline average weights 12 unequal datasets whose per-dataset table is missing here.
- **This copy has no tables or figures**, so results 3 and 4 rest on prose descriptions of Figs. 6, 7,
  9, 10 — and those two readings disagree (result 3).
- **Nothing needs training** — the methods are usable as described, the one clean point in its favour.

## Briefing check

The note: *"extract when long context beats retrieval and at what cost; both studies are QA, not code,
so transfer to plans and diffs is the open question."*

**Supported, with one correction.** The "when" half is well served: "LC generally outperforms RAG …
especially for Wikipedia-based questions. Summarization-based retrieval performs comparably to LC,
while chunk-based retrieval lags behind. However, RAG has advantages in dialogue-based and general
question queries" (abstract), at 56.3 % vs 49.0 % overall (§5.2). **The "at what cost" half is not
supported — this paper measures no cost at all.** Its only cost sentence is a survey row about another
paper (Self-ROUTE), and §6.3's "Computational Cost" is about experiment budgets, not inference.

The note's caveat is understated: it is not only QA rather than code, but a *fixed, non-agentic* RAG
arm, so neither arm resembles our agents' on-demand reading. On the lead's transfer question the paper
**does** supply the axes — naturally segmented sources and open/general questions favour retrieval;
cohesive dense sources and fact-based wh-questions favour reading whole — but its own two reports of
where *papers/reports* and *stories* fall contradict each other, and that ambiguous middle is exactly
where plans, done-records and diffs live. Take the axes as hypotheses to test on our instruments, not
as a routing rule to adopt.
