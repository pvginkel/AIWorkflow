## Core results

1. **Premature termination: long trajectories fail by giving up, not by running out of window.**
   Across four open-source flagship models (GLM-4.7, GLM-5.0, Qwen3.5-397B-A17B, MiniMax-M2.5) on
   three benchmarks, as trajectory length grows, accuracy drops sharply and the *dominant* failure
   mode shifts from confident-incorrect (early) to uncertain-incorrect / give-up (late) — while a
   large share of the 200–256K window is still free (Fig. 2, §3.3). Terminal-state shares on
   BrowseComp (Table 2, %): Qwen3.5 — confident-correct 32.8, uncertain-incorrect 33.6, give-up
   19.8, no-answer 0.0; GLM-4.7 — 29.8 / 19.6 / 38.6 / 0.0; MiniMax-M2.5 — 34.2 / 48.2 / 5.2 / 0.0.
   On the easier xbench-DeepSearch, give-up is 4.4–11.6 %. So the rot mode is benchmark-dependent
   and severe where the task is hard.

2. **The correlation survives difficulty control.** For each query they sample five trajectories,
   rank them by length, and form five groups by rank (group *i* = the *i*-th longest trajectory of
   every query), holding the query set — hence difficulty — fixed while mean length rises. The
   premature-termination rate rises monotonically from shortest to longest group across datasets and
   models (Fig. 4, §3.3). **This is the paper's central causal-ish claim; it is delivered as a figure
   with no printed numbers.** The shape of degradation with length is therefore qualitative in this
   paper: "rises with length", no slope, no knee.

3. **Struggle predicts the failure.** Steps are labelled `struggle` / `not struggle` by an LLM judge
   (91.4 % human agreement, n=198 steps); give-up and uncertain-incorrect trajectories have
   systematically higher struggle scores (Fig. 3). Again figure-only, no numbers.

4. **Seven context-management methods, all of them test-time scaling.** Table 3 — accuracy /

   datasets by me from the per-dataset cells; the **Overall** accuracy column is the paper's:

   Every method beats ReAct on accuracy and cuts PT (32.5 → 2.1–20.8 for Qwen3.5), and every method
   costs 1.7–3.2× the tool calls. The paper's framing: context management *is* test-time scaling —
   it buys accuracy by lowering peak context so the model keeps exploring (§4.1, finding 1).

5. **The winner is model-dependent (§4.1, finding 2).** For the stronger agentic model (Qwen3.5)
   sub-agent isolation wins outright (64.9, +10.5). For the weaker (GLM-4.7) FoldAgent is the
   *worst* of the seven (57.5, +5.7) and the paper names keep-latest (w/ sum.) as the best
   accuracy/cost balance. Cost-per-point (my column, derived) tells a blunter story: **trimming
   (discard-all) is the cheapest gain for both models** — 0.54 and 0.74 accuracy points per extra
   tool call vs 0.14–0.35 for the summarizers.

6. **More aggressive management → less rot, more cost, more unfinished runs, small accuracy gain.**
   Threshold sweeps on BrowseComp/Qwen3.5, 64K → 32K trigger:
   *summary (length)* (Table 4) 41.7 → 57.7 tool calls (+38 %) for acc 45.0 → 46.6 (+1.6), PT 20.2 →
   2.4, **NA 16.0 → 38.0**; *discard-all* (Table 6) 29.8 → 40.8 tools (+37 %) for acc 41.8 → 44.6
   (+2.8), PT 41.2 → 19.4, NA 1.8 → 17.0; *keep-latest (w/ sum.)* (Table 7) 40.4 → 46.8 tools
   (+16 %) for acc 44.2 → 48.2 (+4.0), PT 34.2 → 16.2, NA 3.4 → 17.6. The NA column is the hidden
   bill: driving rot to near-zero converts give-ups into *runs that hit the 100-turn budget*.

7. **A cheap terminal-state judge predicts correctness, and filtering on it is worth 2.6–4.9 pts.**
   The GPT-OSS-120B judge agrees with human labels 98.7 % (n=300). Using the "confident answer"
   label alone to predict correctness: F1 0.852 / 0.879 / 0.809 on BrowseComp / BrowseComp-Plus /
   xbench (precision 0.779 / 0.981 / 0.692, recall 0.939 / 0.796 / 0.975 — Table 8). Dropping
   give-up and uncertain trajectories before aggregating 8 parallel samples (Table 5, averages):
   Qwen3.5 fewest-turns 63.1→68.0 (+4.9), min-length 65.5→68.8 (+3.3), majority-vote 64.9→69.4
   (+4.5); GLM-4.7 +4.1 / +3.2 / +2.6. Where rot is mild (xbench) plain ReAct + filtered parallel
   sampling matches or beats the best context management at equal tool calls (Fig. 5).

## Method and setting

Deep-search QA, ReAct scaffold, tools `search` (top-10 per query) / `visit` (page fetch, summarized
by Qwen3-30B) / `finish`; BrowseComp-Plus swaps the web for a local corpus with a Qwen3-Embedding-8B
retriever. **Max 100 interaction turns**, full native context (200K, 256K for Qwen3.5), 5 repeats
per configuration; BrowseComp is a 100-query split. Models: four open-source flagships for the
diagnosis, **only two (Qwen3.5-397B-A17B, GLM-4.7) for the seven-method comparison**. Nothing is
trained — every method is scaffolding, and both judges are prompted, not fine-tuned. Cost is
reported *only* as tool-call count (summarization calls issued by the main agent are counted in it);
there is no token accounting, no wall-clock, no dollars, and no caching anywhere in the cost model.

## Relevance to P1–P4

**P1 (context is the cost).** Only obliquely — the paper never counts tokens or money. What it does
establish is the *direction*: every one of the seven mitigations increased total work by 1.7–3.2×.
Context management is a spend, not a saving, and in our setting the penalty is worse than theirs,
because compaction or trimming rewrites the prefix and voids the cached read at 0.1×.

**P2 (sessions grow; the longest dominate).** The closest fit in the corpus. Their horizon — up to
100 turns and 200–256K context — brackets ours (20–200 turns, median ~80k, one doc-writer at 438k /
192 turns). Result 2 says that at fixed difficulty, the longer trajectory is the *worse* one, so our
longest sessions are not merely the most expensive, they sit in the regime where these models hedge
and quit. Transfer gap: their failure signature is a visible give-up on a QA answer; a code-writer
that quietly under-implements a phase produces no such marker, and "confident incorrect" (8–48 % of
their trajectories) is exactly the mode a give-up detector misses.

**P3 (every session rebuilds the picture).** Partly. Context isolation via sub-agents is the best
method for the stronger model (+10.5) and near-worst for the weaker (+5.7) — consistent with using
Explore sub-agents under Opus producers, though the paper's principle is keyed to *model strength*,
not role, which would argue for sub-agents in our code-writers too (currently excluded by choice).
Beyond that the paper is silent on our actual P3: all seven methods act *within* one trajectory.
Nothing here measures the cost of a fresh session re-reading a 74 KB plan, and no method carries
knowledge from one run to the next.

**P4 (what smaller reads cost in grounding).** The most useful evidence we have, and it points the
opposite way to the worry: across three threshold sweeps, *shrinking* the retained context raised
accuracy (+1.6, +2.8, +4.0 points going 64K→32K) — grounding did not degrade, cost and unfinished
runs went up instead. Caveat before we lean on it: in deep search the discarded material is stale
SERP text whose value has already been extracted, whereas a code-writer's dropped reads are file
contents it may need verbatim. And the *method* to detect when to shrink does not pay: the
struggle-based semantic trigger never beat a dumb turn or length counter (Qwen3.5 59.3 vs 60.7;
GLM-4.7 60.3 vs 60.4).

## Interventions this paper supports

- **Classify each session's terminal state with a cheap judge over its final reasoning** (give-up /
  uncertain / confident / no-answer), and treat "uncertain" as a rework predictor feeding P4's
  instrument set. Rests on 98.7 % human agreement and F1 0.81–0.88 for confident→correct (Tables 8,
  §3.2). Expected: a leading indicator on the blocking-finding and gate-red rates we already track.
  Loses nothing operationally; misses confident-incorrect entirely (their largest single error class
  on some model/benchmark pairs).
- **Cap or split the longest sessions — the doc-writer above all.** Result 2 (fixed difficulty, PT
  rises with length) says the 192-turn/438k session is in the degraded band. Expected direction:
  fewer hedged doc passes. Risk the paper does not measure: our hand-off across a cut costs a full
  33k prefix plus re-reads, and cross-phase coherence in a diff-wide doc pass has no analogue here.
- **If we ever add trimming, trim rather than summarize, and trigger it on turns or length, not on a
  struggle detector.** Discard-all gave the best accuracy per extra tool call for both models (0.54,
  0.74 derived); summary (semantic) matched or lost to summary (turn)/(length) on both. Loses: at
  aggressive thresholds trimming pushes runs into the turn budget (NA 1.8 → 17.0).
- **Keep sub-agent isolation where the producer is our strongest model.** FoldAgent's +10.5 on
  Qwen3.5 vs +5.7 on GLM-4.7 (§4.1). Since every producer of ours is Opus, the paper's rule would
  extend sub-agents *further* than we do today. Loses: the sub-agent returns a summary only — the
  same grounding risk we already accept for plan-writer and doc-writer, unmeasured here for code.
- **Retry-and-filter as an alternative to context surgery on phases where rot is mild.** Filtered
  parallel sampling matched the best context management at equal tool calls on xbench (Fig. 5).
  Expensive in our setting (each retry re-pays the prefix), so this is a fallback, not a default.

## Applicability caveats

- **No Claude, by design.** Footnote 2: closed-source models "encrypt the reasoning content", so
  GPT-5.4 and Claude Opus 4.7 were excluded. Every number is from open-weights models, and the one
  actionable selection rule is *keyed to model strength* — the axis on which our setup sits off the
  end of their range.
- **Not code.** The Limitations section says outright it is unclear whether these error
  distributions generalise "to other long-horizon agentic domains like software development".
  Benchmarks are exact-match retrieval QA with a single answer; our phases have a deterministic gate
  and a diff, not an answer string.
- **Cost = tool calls.** No tokens, no dollars, no caching. Our economics (82 % cache) can invert
  their ranking: their cheapest method by tool count may be our most expensive by cache
  invalidation.
- **The 100-turn cap is load-bearing.** Much of the NA blow-up (up to 38 %) is an artefact of that
  budget interacting with compaction. Our loops have no such hard cap.
- **The two headline diagnosis results are figures without numbers** (Figs. 2–4). The degradation
  shape is directionally established, not quantified — do not cite a slope from this paper.
- **Everything is within-trajectory.** No result bears on files-as-memory, fresh-session hand-offs,
  or repeated re-reading across sessions.

## Briefing check

The note — *"seven context-management methods compared on performance, cost and rot; open-source
models only"* — is accurate on all four counts, and the open-source restriction is stated by the
authors themselves ("Our evaluation focuses exclusively on open-source models", Limitations).
Three qualifications:

1. **"cost" means tool calls.** Table 3's cost column is `# Tool`, with the agent's own
   summarization calls folded in. There is no token or dollar figure in the paper, so it cannot rank
   methods on our cost basis.
2. **The seven-method comparison covers two models, not four.** GLM-5.0 and MiniMax-M2.5 appear only
   in the diagnosis (Table 2). The headline "best method is model-dependent" therefore rests on a
   two-point comparison.
3. **"rot" is operationalised narrowly** as the premature-termination rate — the give-up plus
   uncertain-incorrect share. It is not a general accuracy-decay metric, and the paper's own
   Limitations doubt it generalises to software development. A method that cuts PT to 2.1 while
   moving accuracy +4.9 (summary-length on Qwen3.5) shows how loosely the rot metric and the
   performance metric are coupled.
