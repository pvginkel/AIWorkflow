## Core results

1. **Holding the prompt as a REPL variable instead of in the context window is the load-bearing
   change.** An RLM initialises a Python REPL with the prompt bound to a `context` variable plus an
   `llm_query` (and at depth>1 `rlm_query`) function, and gives the root model *only constant-size
   metadata* — length, a short prefix, chunk lengths. Every REPL `stdout` is likewise truncated to a
   prefix + length before being appended to the root's history (§2). Ablation (Obs. 2): the REPL
   alone — RLM(depth=0), no sub-calls — already scales past the base model's window and beats every
   non-REPL task-agnostic baseline on most long-context settings; **on LongBench-v2 CodeQA with
   Qwen3-Coder-480B, depth=0 beat every sub-calling RLM variant.** Recursion adds value specifically
   on information-dense inputs.

2. **Measured margins on GPT-5, median across the four benchmarks (Abstract):** +26 % vs a
   compaction agent, +130 % vs CodeAct with sub-calls, **+13 % vs Claude Code**. The ordering matters
   more than the magnitudes: the scaffold that already reads through tools/files (Claude Code, Claude
   Opus 4.1, v2.0.0) is by far the strongest baseline; summarisation and verbalised sub-calls are the
   weak ones.

3. **Per-benchmark, prose-cited:** OOLONG (linear-complexity aggregation) — RLM(depth=1) beats the
   base model by 28.4 % (GPT-5) and 33.3 % (Qwen3-Coder). OOLONG-Pairs (quadratic) — base GPT-5 and
   Qwen3-Coder score ≤ 0.1 % F1; RLM(depth=1) scores 58.0 % and 23.1 %. BrowseComp-Plus (1K docs,
   6–11 M input tokens) — RLM(GPT-5, depth=1) beats the compaction and retrieval baselines by
   **> 29 %** at an **average $0.99 per task**, against a linearly extrapolated $1.50–$2.75 for
   GPT-5-mini simply ingesting the corpus (Obs. 1). In the 20-task scaling appendix (§12.2),
   RLM(GPT-5) is the only method holding perfect accuracy at 1000 documents; the depth=0 ablation
   holds ~90 %; every direct-context GPT-5 variant degrades. RLM cost there scales log-linearly.

4. **Cost is comparable, but the distribution is long-tailed.** Obs. 4 / Fig. 11: the **median** RLM
   run is *cheaper* than the median base-model run, but the **mean** is higher, because of outlier
   trajectories where the RLM flails. Fig. 14–15 histograms are explicitly "long-tailed,
   high-variance". Runtimes are worse still — all sub-LM calls in their implementation are blocking
   and sequential (Appendix 10, "RLMs without asynchronous LM calls are slow").

5. **Degradation is a function of task complexity, not just length** (Obs. 3, Fig. 1). Across
   2^13→2^20 tokens on S-NIAH (O(1) work), OOLONG (linear), OOLONG-Pairs (quadratic), GPT-5 degrades
   faster the denser the task; the RLM degrades more slowly and **wins consistently past 2^14 tokens
   (~16k)** — i.e. the crossover is low, not at the context limit.

6. **The first decomposition dominates, and it is promptable.** Fig. 4(a): varying the in-context
   decomposition examples in the RLM system prompt changes both the first decomposition attempt and
   final OOLONG accuracy — **"even if the example is unrelated to the actual task"** (§5). Numbers
   are in the dropped figure. On LongCoT-mini, adding an explicit decompose-and-delegate hint
   (verbatim in Appendix 11.3: plan a DAG, memoise verified node answers in a dict, verify before a
   child consumes a value, never trust values from earlier turns) gives RLM(GPT-5.2, depth=1)
   **+69.5 % over base GPT-5.2** overall.

7. **Failure modes are scaffold-shaped, not model-shaped.** RLM(Qwen3-Coder) makes far more syntax
   errors than RLM(GPT-5), which is why *higher* recursion depth makes Qwen3-Coder *worse*
   (§5, Fig. 4b). A documented failed trajectory ($1.12, OOLONG-Pairs Q3): the model built the
   correct answer into a REPL variable on step 1, then **discarded it, re-derived it five times, and
   finally returned a root-LM-generated wrong answer instead of the variable** (§13.2). Qwen3-Coder
   fired "hundreds to thousands" of sub-calls on a task GPT-5 solved in ~ten (§13.3). Returning a
   *variable* vs a *generated answer* (`FINAL_VAR` vs `FINAL`) was brittle: in the training corpus,
   16 % of turns misused `FINAL` and 13 % misused `FINAL_VAR` (Appendix 9).

8. **Trainable (outside our surface).** RLM-Qwen3-8B — rejection-fine-tuned on 1,000 filtered
   RLM(Qwen3-Coder) trajectories from an *unrelated* domain — gains a median 28.3 % across the four
   tasks, at lower cost and >3× faster (Obs. 6).

## Method and setting (what was actually built/tested)

Inference-time scaffold, **prompted not trained** for all headline results; training is a separate
small-scale exercise. Root model: GPT-5 (medium reasoning) or Qwen3-Coder-480B-A35B; sub-calls for
the GPT-5 runs use **GPT-5-mini** (an explicit cost/quality choice). Depths 0–3. Tasks are all
**question-answering over a supplied corpus**, single-answer, automatically scored: S-NIAH,
BrowseComp-Plus (1K docs), OOLONG `trec_coarse`, OOLONG-Pairs (20 synthetic queries), LongBench-v2
CodeQA (multiple-choice repo comprehension), plus LongCoT-mini. Horizon: RLM turn counts in the tens
(max 20 iterations in the RL setup); sub-call counts range from ~10 (GPT-5 on OOLONG) to ~500
(Qwen3-Coder correct OOLONG rollouts). Baselines: base model; compaction agent (chunk-and-summarise
when full — GPT-5-nano does the summarising for the GPT-5 arm, to keep cost sane); CodeAct with BM25
and CodeAct with a sub-call tool; OpenCode and **Claude Code (Claude Opus 4.1, v2.0.0)** in two
variants — context offloaded to a file, and context pasted into the initial prompt. Task-agnostic
throughout: one fixed system prompt per method across all tasks. Harness and benchmarks are
open-source (github.com/alexzhang13/rlm); the frontier models are not.

## Relevance to P1–P4

**P1 (context is the cost).** The paper's whole thesis is that input volume is the cost *and* the
quality ceiling, and that the fix is to never put the artefact in the window: hand the model a
handle plus metadata, and let it fetch slices. The BrowseComp number is the only clean cost
comparison — $0.99 for an RLM against $1.50–$2.75 for merely ingesting the same corpus, at +29 %
accuracy. Transfer gap: their cost is uncached API tokens; our 82 % is *cache read/write*, at 0.1×
and 1.25×. A programmatic reader trades cheap cache reads of a stable prefix for expensive fresh
sub-call prefixes, so their cost ratio does not carry over arithmetically.

**P2 (sessions grow; the longest dominate).** Directly on point for the doc-writer: one session,
whole shipped diff, 192 turns, median 145k/turn, one at 438k. That is OOLONG-shaped — linear
semantic work over the whole artefact, where RLM(depth=1) beat the base model by 28.4 % on GPT-5 —
and the crossover past which the programmatic reader wins is only ~2^14 tokens. The counterweight is
Obs. 4: the *median* run gets cheaper, the *mean* gets worse from tail trajectories, and their tails
are exactly the "re-derive it five times" pattern.

**P3 (every session rebuilds the same picture).** The paper is silent on cross-session reuse — one
RLM invocation, one prompt, no memory between tasks; nothing about a fresh session re-reading a 74 KB
plan. The one applicable transfer is *within* a session: their stdout rule (root sees prefix + length
only, full value stays in a variable) is a shape our tool wrappers could copy.

**P4 (unknown grounding cost of smaller reads).** The best evidence in the paper *against* naive
shrinking is the compaction result (+26 % for RLM over compaction on GPT-5) and the framing that
compaction "presumes that *some* details that appear early in the prompt can safely be forgotten" and
"is rarely expressive enough for tasks that require dense access". Note the polarity: this is evidence
that *summarising* loses grounding, and that *slicing with retained full access* does not — the RLM
never destroys the artefact, it just doesn't ship it into the window. There is no coding-task
grounding measurement here at all.

## Interventions this paper supports

- **Give the doc-writer a programmatic reader over the diff instead of the diff.** Rests on Obs. 1–3
  (OOLONG +28.4 %; crossover at ~16k tokens; §13.4's CodeQA trajectory reading a 900k-token repo for
  $0.27). Direction: lower per-turn context on our single largest session; unknown effect on doc
  quality. Loses: whole-diff coherence — a chunked reader can miss a cross-file consequence unless
  an aggregation pass sees all the buffers.
- **Shape tool output like the RLM REPL: return a handle plus prefix + length, keep the body in a
  file, never dump.** Rests on §2 (constant-size stdout metadata) and Obs. 2 (the REPL, not the
  recursion, is what buys the scale). Cheap, mechanical, fits our wrapper surface. Loses little,
  provided the model can always re-fetch — this is *deferral*, not compaction.
- **Put a worked decomposition example in the dispatch prompt of any role that fans out.** Rests on
  Fig. 4(a): an in-context decomposition example moves both the first attempt and the final score,
  *even when unrelated to the task*; and the LongCoT +69.5 % hint. Our plan-writer, plan-reviewer and
  doc-writer already use Explore sub-agents with no such example.
- **Make sub-agent contracts return a named artefact, not prose, and forbid re-deriving what a
  previous step produced.** Rests on §13.2's $1.12 failure (correct answer built into a variable,
  discarded, re-derived 5×) and the 16 %/13 % FINAL/FINAL_VAR misuse. Direction: kills tail cost.
- **Cap and batch sub-calls explicitly in the prompt.** Rests on Appendix 10: they had to add one
  sentence for Qwen3-Coder or it sub-called per line, and on the median-vs-mean cost split. Loses:
  coverage on genuinely dense passages.
- **Do not adopt compaction.** Rests on the +26 % vs compaction result and the §1 argument. Confirms
  the existing "no compaction" setting rather than proposing anything.

## Applicability caveats

- **No Claude as an RLM root anywhere.** Claude Opus 4.1 appears only as the *baseline* inside Claude
  Code. The headline "+13 % vs Claude Code" therefore confounds scaffold with model (GPT-5-based RLM
  vs Opus-based agent) and cannot be read as "this scaffold would beat our loop".
- **QA, not authoring.** Every benchmark has a short, checkable answer. Nothing here measures writing
  code, editing a repo, or producing a long correct document — precisely what our writers do. CodeQA
  is the nearest neighbour and is multiple-choice comprehension; on it, notably, *recursion did not
  help* (depth=0 best for Qwen3-Coder).
- **Horizon mismatch.** Tens of RLM iterations vs our 20–200-turn tool loops; single-shot tasks vs a
  30-session slice. No cross-session or memory findings.
- **Caching absent.** No prompt caching in any cost figure; our economics are dominated by it.
- **Benchmark quirks.** OOLONG-Pairs is 20 hand-written synthetic queries built to force quadratic
  work; the ≤ 0.1 % → 58 % swing is the most spectacular number in the paper and the least
  representative of real work.
- **Table 1 unavailable in this copy** (see frontmatter) — per-method cost and accuracy cells,
  Fig. 4's ablation magnitudes and Fig. 11's quartiles cannot be quoted.

## Briefing check

The note — *"prompt as external environment; compared against compaction and sub-call scaffolds"* —
is accurate and slightly under-sells the paper. "Prompt as external environment" is the authors' own
framing verbatim (§1: long prompts "should instead be treated as *part of the environment that the
LLM is tasked to symbolically and recursively interact with*"). Both comparisons exist and are
quantified: "on GPT-5 by a median across the evaluated benchmarks of 26 % against compaction, 130 %
against CodeAct with sub-calls" (Abstract). What the note omits is the third comparison, which is
the one that matters most to us: **Claude Code is also a baseline, and it is the strongest one — the
margin against it is 13 %, half the margin against compaction and a tenth of the margin against
verbalised sub-calls.** A tool-driven coding agent reading files is already most of the way to an
RLM; the residual is programmatic recursion on information-dense aggregation. Nothing in the note is
wrong; read it as "compaction loses badly, verbalised sub-calls lose worse, our own scaffold class
loses least".

**Transfer answer for the lead:** yes, but narrowly. The evidence supports a role reading a large
artefact through a handle rather than in full — Obs. 2 shows the offload is what buys the scale, and
Fig. 1 puts the crossover at ~16k tokens. It does **not** show that recursive sub-calls help on
code-comprehension work (CodeQA: depth=0 won), and it offers no measurement on artefact *authoring*
or on any Claude model as the reader.
