# Judging the Judges: A Systematic Study of Position Bias in LLM-as-a-Judge

Lin Shi, Chiyu Ma, Wenhua Liang, Xingjian Diao, Weicheng Ma, Soroush Vosoughi — Dartmouth College

## Abstract

LLM-as-a-Judge is a promising substitute for human evaluators, but inherent biases — especially **position bias**, the tendency to favor a solution because of where it sits in the prompt rather than its content — undermine its reliability. This exploratory study measures position bias in LLM judges across both pairwise and list-wise comparison settings, introducing three metrics: repetition stability, position consistency, and preference fairness. The experiments span 15 LLM judges, two benchmarks (MTBench and DevBench), 22 tasks, and roughly 40 solution-generating models, producing over 150,000 evaluation instances. The authors identify Judge-Level, Candidate-Level, and Task-Level factors that contribute to bias. Position bias is confirmed to not be random chance, and it varies significantly across judges and tasks. It is weakly influenced by the length of prompt components but strongly affected by the quality gap between the compared solutions. An agreement/disagreement analysis among judges gives further insight into how judging difficulty is distributed across a dataset and how that could inform dataset design.

## 1. Introduction

Human evaluation is the gold standard for judging LLM outputs but does not scale, which motivated LLM-as-a-Judge as an automated alternative. Despite generally high agreement with human judgments, LLM judges carry systematic biases, and position bias — favoring a candidate answer because of its slot in the prompt rather than its quality — is the paper's focus. The authors argue prior work treated position bias only in passing, alongside other biases, without a focused and systematic study, and that most empirical work looked only at pairwise settings, leaving list-wise comparison largely unexamined. They also note that existing mitigation strategies tend to be incomplete, add complexity, introduce new biases, work inconsistently, or (for internals-based fixes) be inapplicable to closed-source models.

Building on the existing Position Consistency metric, the paper introduces two new metrics, Preference Fairness and Repetition Stability, and organizes bias-contributing factors into three levels: Judge-Level, Candidate-Level, and Task-Level. Experiments are primarily pairwise (where LLM judges perform best) and are extended to list-wise comparisons of 3+ candidates.

Headline findings stated in the introduction:
1. Position bias in capable LLM judges is not a result of random variation.
2. The *direction* of preference (primacy vs. recency) is highly volatile, even for the same judge across different tasks.
3. Differences in answer quality between candidates significantly influence position consistency.
4. Position bias correlates very weakly with the length of the prompts generated by candidate models.

A follow-on agreement analysis across judges shows that even judges with similar aggregate consistency/fairness scores disagree substantially on individual instances; instances with broad agreement are "easy to judge" while high-disagreement instances are the hardest and most prone to position bias. The authors suggest this could guide future benchmark design (mixing easy/hard instances deliberately) and could let a future difficulty-predictor flag hard-to-judge instances before running LLM judges on them.

## 2. Evaluation Settings & Definitions

### 2.1 Pairwise & List-wise Comparison

**Pairwise:** the judge sees a system prompt, a task question, and two candidate solutions (identities hidden — double-blind) and picks the better one. The same prompt is then re-run with the two solutions' positions swapped, producing a "judgment pair." A judge is *position consistent* if it picks the same winner regardless of position; otherwise it displays position bias, directionally classified as *primacy* (favors whichever solution is shown first) or *recency* (favors whichever is shown last). Two prompting modes are used: **Two-Option** (forced choice between A/B) and **Three-Option** (adds a tie option C).

**List-wise:** three or more candidates are judged at once and the judge picks only the single best (not a full ranking), for efficiency. The pairwise "swap" generalizes to full order permutations — for *p* candidates, *p* permutations are run so each candidate occupies the first slot exactly once. A tie/no-clear-winner option is included here too.

### 2.2 Evaluation Metrics

- **Repetition Stability (RS):** how consistently a judge answers the *identical* query across repeated trials (same order, same everything) — i.e., is the judge's decision process actually reproducible, or effectively noisy. Computed as the average, across queries, of the most frequent choice's share of repeated trials. Ranges from a low value (near-random) to 1.0 (perfectly stable). High RS is treated as a prerequisite for trusting PC/PF numbers as reflecting real bias rather than noise.
- **Position Consistency (PC):** the fraction of judgment series (across position permutations) where the judge picks the same winner regardless of position. This is the metric used by prior position-bias work (e.g., Zheng et al.'s LLM-as-a-Judge study).
- **Preference Fairness (PF):** a new metric the paper proposes. Prior approaches to quantifying primacy/recency bias either (a) normalize primacy/recency counts by total dataset size — sensitive to dataset size and hard to compare across datasets — or (b) normalize only over the *inconsistent* subset — which unfairly penalizes highly-consistent judges by ignoring that consistent judgments are inherently fair. PF combines both: it is a min–max-scaled score built from primacy-count, recency-count, and their respective rates among inconsistent judgments, scaled per judge/task so it is comparable across datasets. PF = 0 means preference-fair; PF > 0 means recency-preferred; PF < 0 means primacy-preferred; PF = ±1 means position-consistency is zero and *all* disagreements go one direction. For list-wise settings PF is computed "one vs. all" (first position = primacy, everything else = recency).

### 2.3 Factors Affecting Position Bias

Factors are grouped as Judge-Level (model "family" — GPT / Claude / Gemini / Llama, used as a proxy for size/training since those details aren't public for closed models), Candidate-Level and Task-Level (**answer quality gap**, task input/output length, prompt length). The paper defines a new **answer quality gap (δq)**: judgments are split into consistent wins, consistent ties, and inconsistent series; an **overall win rate (owr)** is computed treating inconsistent series as partial ties, and δq = |owr − 1/p| where 1/p is the "everything's a tie" baseline for a p-candidate list. This overall-win-rate approach (versus using only the consistent-win subset, as prior work did) is argued to better capture "comparable quality" cases where similar-quality answers are exactly where position bias tends to appear.

## 3. Experiment

**Judges:** 15 models — 7 GPT (o1-mini, GPT-4o, GPT-4-Turbo, GPT-4, GPT-3.5-Turbo, and others), 4 Claude (3.5-Sonnet, 3-Opus, 3-Sonnet, 3-Haiku), 3 Gemini (1.5-pro, 1.0-pro, 1.5-flash), and 3 Llama (3.3-70B, 3.1-405B, 3.1-8B).

**Benchmarks:** MTBench (30 candidate models, 8 tasks — coding, extraction, humanities, math, reasoning, roleplay, stem, writing — 10 questions/task; baseline candidate = vicuna-13b-v1.3; Two-Option mode) and DevBench (10 candidate models, 14 tasks derived by splitting DevBench's "general" metric into UML-class, UML-sequence, and architecture-design sub-metrics, 8 questions/task; baseline = human; Three-Option mode). List-wise experiments sampled 9 models into three 3-candidate lists and ran 4 representative judges on MTBench.

**Scale:** RS was computed from 3 questions × 4 candidates × 3 repeated trials per judge (576 instances/judge on MTBench, 432 on DevBench, temperature = 1). PC/PF were computed over the full datasets (4,800 instances on MTBench, 2,240 on DevBench per judge). In total, over 100,000 (per the main text; the abstract says 150,000+ once list-wise and repeated runs are counted) evaluation instances were analyzed.

**Statistical method:** bidirectional stepwise regression (AIC-based forward/backward selection) tested average input/output/prompt length, answer quality gap, judge identity, candidate identity, and task category as predictors of PC and PF.

## 4. Main Findings

**Position bias in capable judges is not random.** Judges with low error rates generally show RS > 0.85; the strongest (Claude-3.5-Sonnet, GPT-4, Llama-3.3-70B) exceed 0.95 on both benchmarks. This is treated as confirming that a single run's judgment (and its position bias) reflects a real, repeatable evaluation pattern rather than noise.

**Position bias varies by judge and by task.** GPT-4o is more position-consistent than GPT-4 on coding tasks but less consistent elsewhere; Gemini-1.5-pro's PC advantage over other Gemini models narrows specifically on extraction tasks. Preference direction (primacy vs. recency) also flips by dataset for the same model: GPT-4 and GPT-3.5-Turbo are recency-preferred on MTBench but primacy-preferred on DevBench; Claude-3.5-Sonnet is nearly fair on MTBench (PF = 0.01) but notably recency-preferred on DevBench (PF = 0.22). Higher PC correlates with better PF on average (a regression trend the paper shows via scatterplot), but the relationship is not tight — some judges keep the same skewed preference direction across tasks even at high PC. In list-wise comparisons the same judge-and-task-dependence appears; more capable judges (GPT-4o, Claude-3.5-Sonnet) hold up better moving from pairwise to list-wise, while GPT-3.5-Turbo degrades more.

**Position bias correlates strongly (parabolically) with the answer quality gap (δq).** As δq grows — i.e., as one candidate is clearly better or clearly worse than baseline — PC rises; when candidates are near quality-equal, PC drops (position bias becomes more likely). PF improves with δq too, but less sharply, and the effect is judge-dependent: GPT-family PF stays near 0 across the quality-gap range in the paper's plots, while Claude and Gemini-pro PF shows a clearer trend with δq. The authors explicitly flag exceptions are common — δq is an important but not sole driver.

**Position bias is only weakly length-dependent.** Of task-input length, task-output length, and full-prompt length, stepwise regression found only average task-output length a (weakly) significant predictor — of PF only, with a small AIC improvement — contradicting the intuitive idea that longer answers systematically bias judges' position choices.

**LLM agreement/disagreement analysis.** Beyond aggregate PC/PF, the paper measures how often pairs of judges agree on the *same instance*, and computes a per-instance "disagreement" count (deviation from the modal choice across all judges). On MTBench, over half the dataset is "easy" — over 80% of the 15 judges agree (disagreement ≤ 3) — while fewer than 2% of instances are especially hard (disagreement ≥ 8, i.e., near-even 3-way splits). The appendix's agreement-heatmap analysis further finds "familial" clustering: GPT-4/GPT-4-Turbo/GPT-4o agree >70% (>85% excluding ties); GPT-3.5-Turbo disagrees with the GPT-4 family/o1-mini ~40% of the time; Claude-3-Opus agrees closely with both Claude-3.5-Sonnet and Claude-3-Sonnet, but Claude-3.5-Sonnet and Claude-3-Sonnet do not agree closely with each other; Gemini models show low mutual agreement generally, though the strongest (1.5-pro) aligns more with GPT-4-family/Claude-3-Opus; Llama-3.3-70B and Llama-3.1-405B agree well with each other and the GPT series, while the much smaller Llama-3.1-8B does not track them. Across both benchmarks, at least 75% of instances get consensus among ≥75% of judges, and the highest-disagreement instances are rare (<5%), leading the authors to conclude majority voting across multiple capable judges is likely reliable for >95% of instances. They note hardest instances are characterized by (1) frequent judge disagreement, (2) minimal quality gap between candidates, and (3) pronounced position bias among most judges — and suggest this could inform deliberately difficulty-balanced benchmark construction.

## 5. Conclusion

The study provides an in-depth analysis of position bias across pairwise and list-wise LLM-as-a-Judge settings using three metrics (RS, PC, PF), finding significant judge- and task-level variation, a weak length effect, and a strong answer-quality-gap effect. Agreement/disagreement analysis across judges is presented as informative for future benchmark refinement.

## 6. Limitations (paper's own, Section "Limitations")

- Only 12 closed-source judges were evaluated for pairwise settings and 4 for list-wise, with list-wise capped at 3-candidate lists; only 3 open-source (Llama 3.1+) models were included, and smaller/older open-source models were excluded for insufficient context length.
- Original benchmark prompt templates were used as-is; alternative prompting strategies were not explored.
- Judge-Level factors like architecture/parameter count could not be directly measured for closed models — "familial property" (GPT/Claude/Gemini/Llama grouping) is a proxy, and with only 3 open-source models even that grouping isn't strongly validated for open models.
- All analysis was conducted *post hoc* on completed judgments, not used to steer or filter judgments prospectively.
- The paper's scope is characterizing position bias, not mitigating it; it does not test or propose any debiasing/mitigation method, though it flags the near-equal-quality regime as the highest-priority target for future mitigation work, and notes multivariate interaction effects among length/task-complexity/quality-gap remain unexplored.

## Relation to prior/related work (Appendix, condensed)

The related-work discussion situates the paper against prior debiasing approaches — "inconsistency-as-a-tie" heuristics, bootstrapping, split-and-merge prompting, multi-agent debate, and mechanistic fixes to positional embeddings/attention (the latter inapplicable to closed-source judges since they require model-internals access). It also notes prior work (Wang et al., cited as wang2023large) found conflict/position-bias rate negatively correlated with score gap in score-based judging, which this paper's δq/owr formulation extends to pairwise and list-wise comparative (not just score-based) settings, and argues list-wise position-bias measurement — beyond simple pairwise swap-consistency — was previously underexplored, both because full ranking is combinatorially expensive and because "who does the judge favor across n possible first-place slots" is a harder question than pairwise primacy/recency; this is what motivates the paper's list-wise PC/PF and the "pick the best of n, not a full ranking" framing.
